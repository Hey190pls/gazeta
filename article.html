A black hole 360 million times the Sun’s mass, seen at cosmic dawn

The quasar Dún Aonghasa was already shining when the universe was just 630 million years old. Its discovery sets a new distance record and offers clues to how enormous black holes grew so quickly.

My Gazette science desk·November 24, 2026·4 min read

An original illustration of a quasar: a bright, active galactic core powered by a feeding supermassive black hole.

Astronomers have identified the most distant known quasar, a brilliant galaxy core powered by a supermassive black hole. The object, J1504+6252, is nicknamed Dún Aonghasa after an ancient stone fort on Ireland’s Aran Islands.

The team found it while searching millions of observations from the European Space Agency’s Euclid space telescope. Follow-up observations, including with the James Webb Space Telescope, measured a redshift of 8.13. That places the quasar at a time when the universe was about 630 million years old.

The key numbers

  • 630 million years: the age of the universe when the quasar’s light was emitted.
  • 13.1 billion years: roughly how long that light traveled to Earth.
  • 360 million solar masses: the estimated mass of the central black hole.
  • 40 million years: how much earlier it existed than the previous record-holder.

Why this record matters

Quasars are among the brightest persistent objects in the cosmos. Gas falling toward a black hole heats up and glows, allowing astronomers to study these systems even at great distances. Finding such a massive black hole so early in cosmic history puts pressure on models of how quickly black holes can form and grow.

The research team says the result is consistent with a scenario in which a large gas cloud collapsed directly into a black hole seed, which then gathered more matter. The discovery does not settle the question: future, more distant quasars may reveal whether other formation pathways are needed.

How it was found

Euclid’s wide survey helped flag the candidate. Observations from ground-based observatories and space telescopes then established its distance and black-hole mass. The findings were published in Astronomy & Astrophysics.
